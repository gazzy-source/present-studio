# What Premium Means

Operational definition. Not aspiration — a set of tests an artifact passes or
fails.

Premium is **not** price, logo prominence, minimalism, or the word "luxury."
Those are outputs or costumes. Premium is a set of behaviours that survive
scrutiny.

---

## The five signals

### 1. Restraint

Fewer words, fewer elements, fewer claims, more space. The premium instinct is
subtractive. Mass-market work fills the frame because empty space feels wasted;
premium work leaves it because space is the most expensive thing in the frame.

**Observable:** short sentences. High whitespace. One idea per surface.

### 2. Specificity

Precise nouns, real materials, named processes, exact hours and places. Vagueness
reads as either ignorance or concealment. Both are cheap.

**Observable:** a reader could fact-check the sentence.

### 3. Certainty

No persuasion, no urgency, no justification. Premium brands do not argue for
themselves. Stating and stopping is the entire mechanism.

**Observable:** remove every sentence that tries to convince. If nothing is left,
the work was advertising, not brand.

### 4. Craft evidence

Signals of how something was made: construction, weight, finishing, the decision
someone agonised over. Craft is the receipt for the price.

**Observable:** at least one detail that only someone who made it would know.

### 5. Point of view

A stance the brand holds that some people will not share. Universality is a
mass-market objective. Premium brands are willing to be declined.

**Observable:** the work would alienate a describable group. If it offends no
taste, it has none.

---

## Anti-signals

Any of these present means the artifact is not premium, regardless of how good
the rest is. They are disqualifying, not deductive.

| Anti-signal | Why it breaks |
|---|---|
| Urgency ("don't miss", "last chance", countdowns) | Signals fear of the audience leaving |
| Superlatives ("finest", "ultimate", "best-in-class") | Unverifiable claims replace evidence |
| Self-description as luxury/premium/exclusive | Stating status is the opposite of holding it |
| Exclamation marks | Volume substituting for substance |
| Discount framing as identity | Trains the audience to wait |
| Generic sensory adjectives ("buttery soft", "effortlessly cool") | Category filler, brand-agnostic |
| Explaining the feeling | Replaces experience with instruction |
| Trend-chasing vocabulary | Dates the work to a quarter |
| Stock-photograph situations | The world is borrowed, not built |

---

## The substitution test

**The single most useful gate in the system.**

Take any artifact. Replace the brand name with a competitor's. Does it still
work?

- **If yes, it has failed.** The work is category-generic. Anyone could run it.
- **If no, it has passed.** Something in it is only true of this brand.

Most rejected output fails here and nowhere else. It reads well, breaks no rule,
and belongs to nobody.

---

## Price is a consequence

Premium is not achieved by charging more; charging more is permitted by having
achieved it. The signals above justify price. Price justifies nothing.

An artifact never argues that something is worth its cost. It demonstrates the
conditions under which the question stops being asked.

---

## Premium vs. adjacent modes

| Mode | Mistaken for premium because | Actually differs by |
|---|---|---|
| **Minimal** | Both use space | Minimalism is a visual style; premium is a behaviour. Minimal work can be cheap and often is |
| **Expensive** | Both signal cost | Expense without craft evidence reads as markup |
| **Exclusive** | Both restrict | Exclusivity is a gate; premium is a standard. A standard is held even when nobody is watching |
| **Quiet** | Both are restrained | Quiet without point of view is merely absent |
